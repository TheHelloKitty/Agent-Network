# Crimson Vows: Bound by the Velvet Lantern

## Blurb

To the syndicate ruling the city’s rain-slicked docks, he is Dante—a remorseless enforcer whose name clears streets and settles debts in blood. To the Borelli bloodline, he is Luca, the rightful heir fighting to keep his family's empire from tearing itself apart. He operates in the cold shadows, answering to no one and yielding to nothing.

Until he steps through the blood-red doors of The Velvet Lantern.

Evie Caruso thought she could outrun her family’s tarnished legacy. The Velvet Lantern, a subterranean jazz bar drenched in amber light and smoky secrets, is her sanctuary and her last remaining defense. But when her late father’s lethal debts come due, the predator sent to collect is none other than Luca Dante Borelli himself.

# Chapter 1

The rain over the North Docks fell like liquid iron, drumming a steady, violent cadence against the stained-glass transom of The Velvet Lantern. 

Inside, the world smelled of charred orange peel, aged rye, and damp velvet. A low, bruised-purple spotlight cut through the haze of clove cigarettes, settling over the polished mahogany bar where Evie Caruso wiped down the wood with rhythmic, white-knuckled precision. On the small corner stage, a saxophone groaned out a slow, aching blues progression that vibrated through the floorboards and settled directly beneath her ribs.

The Lantern was subterranean, tucked beneath street level like a buried secret. For three years, since the night her father stopped breathing and left behind a mountain of ashes and unpaid markers, it had been her fortress. 

Tonight, the air felt too thin. 

Evie paused, the damp linen towel bunching in her fingers. The hairs on the nape of her neck rose, an instinct born of growing up in a city where survival depended on reading shadows before they took the shape of men. 

At the back of the room, the heavy oak door groaned open.

The laughter near the brass rail died first. Then the clinking of cut crystal ceased. Even the saxophonist missed

a beat, his brass note souring into silence before he lowered his instrument entirely. 

Cold air rolled down the carpeted stairs, smelling of ozone, expensive wool, and fresh blood. 

He didn’t hurry. He came down the steps with the languid, predatory certainty of a man who owned the foundations the building rested upon. Broad shoulders, carved in charcoal cashmere, filled the stairwell. His coat was damp with the storm, catching the dim glow of the velvet-shaded lamps. Beneath the tailored cut lay the brutal, compact power of the Borelli bloodline—a legacy built on shattered kneecaps and whispered executions.

To the high-society matriarchs and the parish priest who took his envelopes of clean cash, he was Luca. The dutiful eldest son, the polished heir to the shipping yards and import dynasties. 

To the gutter, to the men who bled out in alleyways behind the fish markets, he was Dante. Death with an Italian accent and gray, pitiless eyes.

Evie didn’t move. Her heart thudded against her sternum, heavy and frantic as a trapped bird, but she kept her chin lifted. She had learned long ago that flinching only invited the wolves to bite.

Two soldiers flanked him—hulking silhouettes with their hands tucked inside their lapels—but Dante waved them off with a flick of his gloved fingers. They melted into the shadows near the coat check, cutting off the single exit.

Dante walked straight to the bar. 

Every stride was quiet, cushioned by the plush runner, yet the sound resonated in Evie’s teeth. He stopped directly across from her, the dark mahogany counter the only barrier between them. Up close, he was suffocating. His jaw was lined with dark stubble, sharp enough to cut, and across the high ridge of his left cheekbone ran a thin, silver scar that disappeared into his black hairline. 

His eyes were the color of stagnant river water in winter. They raked over her—from the loose copper curls pinned haphazardly at her crown, down the curve of her throat, to the swell of her chest rising and falling beneath the emerald silk of her blouse. 

"Evangeline," he said. 

His voice was a low, gravelly rasp that drifted through the quiet room like smoke. It made something deep in the pit of her stomach tighten, a treacherous, unbidden coil of heat that terrified her far more than his reputation.

"It’s Evie," she replied, keeping her voice level, devoid of the tremor vibrating in her knees. "And last call was twenty minutes ago, Mr. Borelli."

A ghost of an amused, cruel smirk touched his lips. He peeled off his leather gloves, finger by finger, setting them on the polished wood between them. On his right ring finger sat the heavy gold signet ring bearing the Borelli serpent, the crest slick with rain.

"I don’t answer to last call in this district," Dante murmured. He rested both hands flat on the counter, leaning in just enough that she could scent him—black pepper, cedarwood, and the sharp, metallic tang of iron. "And neither does your father’s debt."

The air left Evie’s lungs. "My father is in the ground. He died three years ago. Whatever he owed—"

"—didn't die with him," Dante interrupted smoothly. He reached into his coat pocket. Evie stiffened, half-expecting steel, but he withdrew a small, folded piece of parchment tied with faded butcher’s twine. He laid it flat, pressing it down with one calloused thumb. "Enzo Caruso had three vices. Bad horses, cheap gin, and borrowing from the wrong family. My family."

"I paid the weekly tribute to Sal," she whispered, her voice tightening with anger. "Every Sunday. Five hundred dollars in cash. I haven't missed a week."

"Sal is dead," Dante stated casually, as though reporting the evening weather. "Found him an hour ago floating near Pier 4. Seems he was skimming. Taking your five hundred, pocketing four, and telling my father you were laughing in our faces."

Evie’s stomach plummeted. The room seemed to tilt beneath her heels. "I didn't know."

"I know you didn't," Dante said, his gaze dropping to her mouth for a fraction of a second before locking back onto her eyes. "If I thought you were in on it, little bird, we wouldn't be talking over drinks. You’d already be in the trunk of my car."

A cold sweat broke out across her ribs. "How much?"

Dante tapped the ledger parchment. "With interest? Two hundred and forty thousand."

"I don't burn good investments, Evangeline," he murmured, his deep timbre vibrating through the quiet hush of the empty bar. "And I don't settle for scrap copper when there’s far more valuable collateral standing right in front of me."

"My name is Evie," she snapped, fury and panic colliding in her chest. "And I'm not collateral. If Sal stole your money, that's Borelli business. I paid my dues."

"Sal paid for his mistake with his life," Dante said, his tone turning razor-sharp, stripping away the casual veneer to reveal the merciless heir to the Five Points syndicate. "Now the ledger balance lands squarely back on the name signed at the bottom. Caruso."

He stood. The movement was fluid, terrifyingly silent for a man of his size. He didn't rush, but in two long strides, he closed the distance between them, trapping her against the polished mahogany counter. The scent of him—smoke, rain, and clean masculine skin—swallowed her whole. 

Evie refused to cower. She planted her feet, chin tilted upward, meeting his chilling gaze head-on even as her heart hammered against her ribs like a trapped bird. 

"I don't have it," she breathed, the words brushing against the cold leather of his lapels. "Kill me if you want. It won't fill your pockets."

Dante’s mouth curved into a slow, dark smirk that sent an electric shiver straight down the curve of her spine. He reached out, his knuckles brushing lightly against the warmth of her jawline. His skin was rough, calloused from triggers and violence, but the contact was deliberate, possessive. Evie jerked slightly at the touch, but she didn't step back.

"Kill you?" Dante chuckled softly, a dark rumble that seemed to reverberate inside her own chest. "A waste of pretty skin and fierce spirit. My father might want your blood on the floor, little bird. But I’m running the streets now."

"Then what do you want, Dante?" 

His hand lingered on her cheek for a fraction of a second longer than business warranted, his thumb tracing the soft contour of her lower lip before dropping back to his side. "The Lantern stays open. You keep pouring drinks, keep running the front. But starting tonight, this place belongs to the Borellis. Every dollar from the taps, every cut from the poker tables in the basement. It goes to me."

"I don't run games in the basement," Evie said, eyes widening. "This is just a jazz bar."
