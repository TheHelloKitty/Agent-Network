CHAPTER 1  
The Last Pint

The Velvet Lantern kept its own weather.

Outside, rain lashed the alley stones, but inside the air hung warm and thick, smelling of spilled rye, lemon peel, and floor wax. Neon from the front window bled across the floorboards in a bruised, slick pink. Evie Caruso worked a dry rag over the mahogany until the grain gleamed under the amber sconces, refusing to break her rhythm. If she stopped moving, she would hear table three breathing.

They sat like men who believed they held the lease. 

They didn't. 

"Last call was twenty minutes ago," she said, eyes on the counter.

A chair leg bit into the floor. Three of Marco Borelli’s street soldiers moved up to the rail, all stiff wool coats, cheap aftershave, and the heavy, casual arrogance of men backed by a bloodline. The one in the center dropped a manila envelope onto the bar. It landed with a dull, dry slap.

"Not last call for you, sweetheart," he said, leaning in until Evie caught the sour reek of tobacco on his tongue. "Marco wants the deed. Fair price, straight cash. You sign, you keep your fingers. You don't, this place becomes a story people tell over drinks down the block."

Evie laid the rag flat. At twenty-eight, she had spent five years learning which threats were smoke and which had powder behind them. The Lantern had belonged to her father before the cancer and the hospital debts gutted him. It was the only honest patch of light left on an avenue the syndicate treated like an open vein.

"You can't buy what isn't for sale," she said quietly. "And you can't scare me with Marco's stationary."

The man grinned, thin and dead. "Nobody said anything about paper."

From the shadows of the corner booth, the man who had nursed a single pour of bourbon for ninety minutes rose to his feet.

Evie had clocked him the moment he stepped in off the avenue: black coat, no tie, shoulders carrying the quiet tension of an unpinned grenade. A scar cut a pale, jagged line along his jawline, stark against stubble. He moved without haste, but the three at the bar went rigid before he’d taken two strides.

"Walk," he said.

Low. Unhurried. The kind of voice that pulled all the oxygen out of a room.

The middle soldier froze, color bleeding from his face. "Dante. Look, Marco told us—"

"I don't care what my brother told you."

*Dante.* The moniker whispered through the south side whenever collections turned bloody. Evie knew the older name, too—the one spoken in low, cautious tones by the market vendors who remembered the mother who’d raised him before the car bomb on Fourth Street.

*Luca.*

Luca Dante Borelli stopped an arm’s length away, bringing with him the sharp bite of ozone, wet wool, and bitter smoke.
