CHAPTER 1
The Last Pint

The Velvet Lantern kept its own weather.

Outside, November rain lashed the alley, slicking the cobblestones black and greasy under the streetlamps. Inside, the air hung warm and thick, smelling of spilled rye, lemon wax, and eighty years of stale smoke trapped in the plaster. Neon from the front window bled across the floorboards in a wet, electric smear of rose.

Evie Caruso scrubbed the same circle into the bar’s mahogany rail until the grain gleamed like pulled sugar. If she stopped moving, she would have to look at the three men parked at table three. If she looked, she would have to listen to the wet, rhythmic sound of their breathing—thick and deliberate, the posture of wolves testing a fence.

They thought the room belonged to them. 

It didn't. Not while her boots were on the floorboards.

"Last call was twenty minutes ago," Evie said, her voice flat, pitched just loud enough to cut through the rain drumming the plate glass. She kept her eyes on the wood.

A chair scraped back, iron feet shrieking against oak. Heavy soles crossed the room. Marco Borelli’s errand boys: sharp wool coats cut too wide in the shoulders, off-the-rack cologne souring with sweat, and eyes that had seen too many broken fingers to care about another.

The middle one—thick neck, grease-slicked hair, teeth stained with cheap tobacco—dropped a heavy manila envelope onto the counter. The slap of paper against wood was deliberate, loud as a gavel.

"Not last call for you, sweetheart," he murmured, leaning his forearms onto the bar. He smelled like clove gum and cold rain. "Don Marco’s offering a clean buy. Fair market. You put your name on the line, you walk out of here with your knuckles intact. You don't, and this dive becomes another bedtime story about a girl who thought she was harder than concrete."

Evie laid her bar rag across the tap handle. She was five-foot-seven in work boots, twenty-eight years old, her dark curls pinned up with brass bobby pins to keep them off her neck in the heat of the taps. Her hands were scarred from bar glass and kitchen burns, steady as stone. Her father had bled his life into these walls before the hospital debt finally choked out his lungs. The Lantern was the only patch of clean light left on a corridor the Borelli family treated like their private abattoir.

"You can’t buy this place with a bullet," she said, finally meeting his gaze.
