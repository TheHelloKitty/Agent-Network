The neon sign hummed outside the heavy oak doors, casting a bruised crimson glow across the rain-slicked pavement of the North End. Inside The Velvet Lantern, the atmosphere was suffocatingly intimate—dim amber sconces, bruised-plum leather booths, and the low, seductive whine of a muted saxophone cutting through the clinking of cut crystal.

Evie Caruso wiped down the scarred mahogany bar, her movements measured, practiced, and carefully defensive. She kept her chin tipped up, though her nerves frayed with every swing of the brass-hinged front door. In this neighborhood, running a speakeasy under the shadow of the five families meant constantly listening for footsteps you couldn't outrun. 

Her father’s unpaid debts had already painted a target on the Caruso name. She could feel it like a cold blade pressed to the nape of her neck.

Then the brass hinges groaned, and the low rumble of bar chatter simply died.

The temperature in the room dropped ten degrees. The air thickened with the scent of ozone, rain-soaked wool, and pure, unfiltered violence.

Evie didn't need to look up to know who

had walked through the door. 

Only one man carried an aura heavy enough to silence an entire room of thieves, degenerates, and crooked cops with nothing more than his shadow. 

Luca Dante Borelli. 

To the bloodline that ruled the city, he was Luca—the prodigal heir, the favored blood, the velvet-gloved architect of a sprawling empire. But to the streets, to the cold alleys and bleeding men who whispered his name like an omen, he was simply Dante. The executioner. The shadow that came before the grave.

Evie Caruso didn’t look up immediately. She kept her hands steady, wiping down the zinc surface of the bar with a rag that had seen better days, though every hair on the nape of her neck stood at attention. The Velvet Lantern had a rhythm—ice clinking in cheap tumblers, hushed betrayals traded in red-leather booths, the low hum of jazz bleeding from a cracked speaker in the corner. But when Luca Dante entered, the rhythm flatlined. Men swallowed their drinks without tasting them; women shrank into the shadows, clutching their pearls or their knives.

She counted his footsteps. Six. Seven. Eight.

He stopped directly across from her. 

The scent arrived before his voice did—expensive bergamot, rain-soaked wool, and the faint, unmistakable tang of gunpowder. It was an intoxicating, lethal cocktail that made the blood drum thick and hot in her veins.

"Pour me something that burns, *piccola*," he murmured.

Evie finally raised her eyes, letting her gaze climb the tailored expanse of his charcoal coat, past the broad shoulders that carried the sins of the Borelli syndicate, up to a throat corded with lean muscle, and finally to his face. Sharp, aristocratic cheekbones, an uncompromising jaw darkened by a shadow of stubble, and eyes the color of smoke over a burning city. 

He was devastating. A masterpiece carved out of brutality.

"We’re out of the top shelf," Evie said, her voice smooth, betraying none of the rabbit-fast thumping in her ribs. She set the rag aside and reached for a heavy glass tumbler. "Unless you consider rotgut bourbon a luxury."

A slow, dangerous smile pulled at the corner of Dante’s mouth. He rested both gloved hands on the zinc bar, leaning in just enough that the heat radiating from his chest crossed the divide between them. "I didn't come here for the quality of your liquor, Genevieve."

"Nobody calls me Genevieve."

"Your father did."

The name hung between them like a blade suspended by a thread. Evie didn't flinch, but her grip on the bottle tightened until her knuckles went pale. She poured two fingers of amber liquid, the amber catching the dim crimson glow of the neon lights overhead. She slid the glass across the scarred metal. It stopped an inch from his fingers.

"My father is dead," she said quietly, holding his piercing gaze. "And his debts died with him."
