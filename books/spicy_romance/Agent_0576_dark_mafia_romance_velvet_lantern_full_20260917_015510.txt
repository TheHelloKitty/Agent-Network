CHAPTER 1



The brass plaque beside the heavy oak door read *The Velvet Lantern* in clean, elegant script, unbothered by the rot of the alley it opened into. Inside, the lounge breathed in shades of oxblood velvet, polished mahogany, and low amber light designed to make every secret look like an indulgence. 

Behind the bar, Evie Caruso wiped down the zinc countertop with steady, rhythmic strokes. The blues quartet on the corner riser was easing through the tail end of a set, the muted trumpet hanging thick in the air alongside the scent of burnt orange peel, expensive bourbon, and cut tobacco.

It was two in the morning on a Tuesday. The tourists had cleared out hours ago. The only men left in the booths were the ones who didn't exist on paper.

A heavy tumbler clinked against the zinc. Evie didn't jump. She picked up a crystal bottle of Rye, turned, and poured two fingers without looking at the face beneath the brim of the charcoal fedora. 

"Closing soon, Sal," she said, her voice smooth, low, and laced with an authority she had fought with teeth and claw to maintain.

"Just waiting on word from uptown, Miss Caruso," the man murmured, sliding a crisp bill across the damp surface. "Things are restless out on the bricks tonight. The Moretti boys are kicking up dust on the border."

Evie’s fingers tightened briefly around the neck of the bottle before she set it back on the tiered mirror shelf. "The Morettis don't cross the river. Not unless they want their throats opened."

"They might be getting brave."

"Brave is just another word for stupid," she countered softly.

Before Sal could answer, the front door opened. The brass bell overhead didn’t ring; the brass catch was caught in a gloved hand, silencing it before it could make a sound. 

A sudden, suffocating quiet washed over the lounge. The trumpet player stumbled over a flat note, swallowed hard, and stopped playing altogether. The double bass died an instant later. Within three heartbeats, the low rumble of private conversations in the booths vanished, replaced by the distinct, frantic shuffling of men preparing to leave through the rear exit.

Evie felt the shift in the air before she looked up. It felt like ozone before lightning hits iron. A deep, prickling heat crawled along the nape of her neck.

He stood framed in the arched doorway, shaking the chill of the Chicago rain from his dark wool overcoat. Broad-shouldered, towering, and carved from the sort of ruthless precision that money couldn't buy, he commanded the room simply by inhaling its oxygen. His dark hair was slicked back, a few stray wet strands clinging to his temples. High cheekbones, a sharp jaw shadowed by stubble, and eyes the color of bruised obsidian.

To the high-society boardrooms and his father's inner circle, he was Luca Borelli—the brilliant, polished strategist who balanced ledgers and controlled shipping lanes with a flick of his fountain pen.
