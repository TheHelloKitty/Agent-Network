Evie Caruso wiped down the mahogany bar with slow, deliberate strokes. Her fingers ached from hours of counting chipped glassware and uncounted losses. The Lantern had belonged to her grandfather, then her father, and now it was simply an anchor dragging her into the harbor floor. Her father had gambled away everything before a stray bullet ended his run three months ago, leaving behind empty bottles, ruined ledgers, and a name written in red ink across the books of the Borelli family.

The brass bell above the heavy oak door did not chime. It rattled once, choked off as the door opened wide enough to admit a draft of freezing sea mist—and the kind of silence that made the three patrons huddled over their draft beers reach for their coats and vanish out the service exit without asking for change.

Evie didn’t run. She didn’t have anywhere left to go.

She set down the linen rag, smoothed the front of her black silk blouse, and lifted her chin as heavy leather soles struck the floorboards.

He walked out of the shadows beneath the mezzanine like he owned the dark itself. Broad-shouldered, tall enough to force her eyes upward, tailored in charcoal wool that bore the dark sheen of rain across the lapels. His jawline was sharp as freshly broken flint, shadowed by dark stubble, and his eyes—the color of smoked whiskey—were fixed entirely on her.

To the high-society christenings and his grandmother’s Sunday dinners, he was Luca. The favored son, the calculating mind. But on the streets between Hanover and the docks, no one dared use that name. To the city, to every debtor praying for mercy,

He stopped three paces from the polished mahogany bar, the scent of expensive tobacco, ozone, and cold rain rolling off him like a physical presence. His gaze dropped briefly, tracing the line of her throat where her pulse leaped against the pale skin, then roved down the black silk blouse before rising back to pin her in place. "Evie," he said. His voice was a low, resonant rumble that vibrated straight through the floorboards and into the soles of her boots. 

"Dante." She kept her tone level, refusing to let the tremble in her chest reach her vocal cords. She gripped the edge of the bar behind her back, her fingers pressing into the cool wood so hard her knuckles ached. "We’re closed."

A faint, dangerous smirk curved one corner of his sculpted mouth. "Your sign says open till two. It’s barely midnight."

"Private event, then," she countered, lifting her chin higher. "And you’re not on the guest list."

Dante stepped forward. He didn't rush. Every movement was slow, deliberate, the unhurried swagger of an apex predator that knew the prey had nowhere left to run. He rested one gloved hand on the brass railing of the bar, leaning in just enough that she could smell the cedarwood and dark bourbon clinging to his skin. 

"I don't need a list, little bird. I own the ground this place sits on." He tilted his head, his dark hair gleaming in the amber glow of the tarnished sconces. "And as of midnight, I own the four walls holding you up."

Evie felt the blood rush to her ears, a furious mixture of terror and a treacherous, involuntary spike of heat pooling low in her belly. "My father's debts died with him."

"Debts don't die, Evie. They collect interest." Dante unbuttoned his overcoat, revealing the crisp white shirt beneath, pulled taut across the massive breadth of his chest, and the leather shoulder holster nestled securely against his ribs. He peeled off his black leather gloves, finger by finger, never breaking eye contact. The sheer domesticity of the gesture against the lethal reality of who he was felt perversely intimate. 

He tossed the gloves onto the counter between them. 

"Your father borrowed two hundred thousand dollars to chase cards in the underground dens beneath the navy yard," Dante murmured, his voice softening into something almost gentle, which made it ten times more terrifying. "He put the Lantern up as collateral. When that wasn't enough, he signed away your name to guarantee the remainder."

Out in the main room, the two capos by the doorway stood as rigid as statues, their eyes fixed strictly ahead, wise enough not to look toward the bar.

He reached across the narrow strip of mahogany, and before Evie could jerk backward, his fingers cupped the curve of her jaw.
